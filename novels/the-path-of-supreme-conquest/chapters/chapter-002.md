# Chapter 2: Three Names

By the time Jian had washed the blood from his leg and changed the binding, the front hall had filled completely.

He sat near the end of the younger generation’s row with his injured leg extended just enough to keep the fresh bandage from pulling. The bundle of Clearbreath Grass rested beside him where he had set it down on entering. No one had told him to remove it. No one had seemed to notice it at all.

At the head of the hall, Shen Guowei broke the county seal.

The patriarch did not hurry. He was a lean man whose hair had gone almost entirely white without softening his face, and even seated he carried the stillness Jian associated with the late stages of Foundation Establishment. When he drew the paper from the lacquered tube, the small sounds in the hall died one after another until Jian could hear the faint scrape of the sheet unfolding.

Shen Yao remained standing against the far wall.

Jian had looked at his father twice since sitting down. Both times Yao’s expression had been unreadable, but the fact that he had not taken his usual place among the adult men told Jian more than his face did. His father had served in Great Liang’s military before the wound that ended his advancement. He knew what a levy meant before Guowei read a word of it.

The patriarch’s eyes moved down the page.

“By order of the Yunling County Office, pursuant to the western defense levy authorized under provincial command, registered cultivation households and affiliated clans are to provide personnel according to assessed obligation.”

A murmur moved through the hall and disappeared under Guowei’s gaze.

He continued.

The language was formal enough to make dying sound administrative. Names were not requested. Numbers were. Cultivation levels were categorized. Reporting dates were fixed. Equipment requirements, exemptions, substitutions, penalties for refusal, and compensation for service were each given their own paragraph. Great Liang had reduced the question of who would leave Qingyuan and who would remain to a schedule stamped twice in red wax.

Jian listened for the part that mattered.

It came near the end.

“The Shen Clan of Qingyuan City is assessed at three cultivators fit for active service. At least two must have reached the Fifth Layer of Qi Refining or above. No more than one may be below the Fourth Layer. All three are to report to the West Garrison for registration on the thirteenth day before the next Azure Mountain selection.”

For a moment Jian thought he had misheard the date.

Thirteen days before selection.

Five days from now.

The ache beneath his ribs returned although his qi had long since settled.

An elder to Guowei’s left cleared his throat. “The order gives little room.”

“It gives enough,” another replied. “We have nineteen registered cultivators of serviceable age.”

“Six whose absence would cut directly into the herb harvest, and every one of those six can still be sent if we are willing to accept the loss.”

“The county will not care about our harvest.”

“We should.”

The discussion began without anyone announcing that it had begun.

Jian knew every voice. Elder Shen Gao oversaw the training courtyard and believed almost every problem could be solved by making someone repeat a drill until their legs failed. Shen Peng managed the family’s northern fields and could calculate the value of a season’s rain more accurately than most merchants could calculate silver. Aunt Shen Qiao, who was not technically an elder but was invited whenever clan finances were discussed, sat with a ledger already open across her knees.

They spoke first of obligations, not people.

The clan had to send three. That fact was never seriously challenged. Refusal would invite fines at best, confiscation or forced seizure of personnel at worst. The question became which three losses the Shen Clan could absorb with the least damage.

Jian watched the choices narrow.

Shen Tao could be sent, but his absence during the western trading season would leave the caravan accounts to men who had already cost the clan silver in smaller negotiations, and Aunt Shen Qiao quietly placed an estimated loss beside his name. Shen Min had reached the Sixth Layer and was eligible as well, but she was already promised into a marriage alliance with the Luo family; sending her west would not make the marriage impossible, only risk two years of negotiation and the herb contracts expected to follow it. The two brothers from the southern fields were considered separately. Either could serve, but between them they kept the tenancy rolls, irrigation rotations, and boundary disputes for nearly a third of the clan’s leased grain land while supporting a widowed mother whose health no longer allowed her to manage the household. Sending one would force the other to carry both men’s work through harvest. Sending both would leave the branch without an adult cultivator and put the southern holdings into hired hands at the worst possible time. Shen Peng named the likely cost before anyone asked. Shen Rui’s name came up once.

Only once.

“No,” Shen Gao said immediately. “He sits Azure Mountain’s selection in eighteen days.”

No one argued.

Jian felt something tighten in his jaw.

He told himself it was reasonable.

Rui had reached the Seventh Layer in spring. His spiritual roots were better. The clan had spent more on his instruction because the return was more likely to justify it. If Azure Mountain accepted one Shen disciple this year, every person in the hall knew Rui was the best chance.

Reason did not make the silence after his name feel any better.

Guowei listened until the room had exhausted the obvious choices. Then he placed three narrow bamboo slips on the table before him.

He wrote the first name.

“Shen Kaiyuan.”

A man in his late twenties bowed from the adult row. Jian knew him mostly from the herb storehouses, broad-shouldered and quiet, Fifth Layer, unmarried, with two younger brothers already working the family plots.

No one objected.

The second slip received another name.

“Shen Wen.”

A cousin from the western branch looked down at his hands. Fourth Layer. Twenty-two. His father had died three winters ago, but his older sister’s husband had recently moved into their household, which meant someone else could cover the small smithy the family operated near the Crafts Ward.

Again, no one objected.

Guowei picked up the third slip.

Jian knew before the brush touched bamboo.

He could not have said why. Perhaps because every argument had been narrowing toward him for the last quarter hour, or because his father had not once looked away from the patriarch.

Guowei wrote carefully.

“Shen Jian.”

His mother’s breath caught somewhere behind him.

Jian looked at the bamboo slip rather than at anyone in the room.

For several heartbeats there was only the awareness of his own leg throbbing beneath the bandage and the smell of Clearbreath Grass beside him.

Then he stood.

The movement pulled at the wound. He ignored it.

“Patriarch,” he said.

Guowei looked up. “Speak.”

No one told him to sit down. That was almost worse. They expected him to argue.

Jian had spent the first half of the discussion working through the same names the elders were. He had reached the same conclusion they had and hated it enough to want an error in their reasoning, simply because it concerned him.

“The clan has spent a year preparing me for Azure Mountain’s selection,” he said. “Medicine, instruction, access to the courtyard, the application fee, the county registration. If I leave in five days, that investment is lost before I am even tested.”

Aunt Shen Qiao glanced down at her ledger.

Guowei did not.

“The open selection returns every two years,” Jian said.

“I know.”

“I will be nineteen before the next one.”

“For open selection,” Guowei said. “Later entry remains possible through merit or recommendation.”

“Neither is something this clan can plan around,” Jian said.

“Nor is admission eighteen days from now,” Guowei replied.

The answer drew the smallest shift from the elders, not agreement so much as recognition that Jian had finally reached the real edge of the argument.

Jian continued before anyone could answer it for him.

“If I enter Azure Mountain, the clan gains more than what I can provide by serving as one Fifth Layer cultivator in a levy. Even as an outer disciple, access to sect instruction, medicine, contacts, and future advancement benefits the family. If the question is what loss the clan can best absorb, sending me before I am tested may cost more than keeping me here.”

This time it was Shen Gao who answered.

“May.”

The single word irritated Jian more than a dismissal would have.

Gao folded his hands inside his sleeves. “You may enter Azure Mountain. Rui may enter Azure Mountain. Neither of you has entered Azure Mountain. We cannot value a possibility as though it has already occurred.”

“Then let me sit the selection first.”

“The reporting date is five days from now,” Aunt Qiao said.

“I know.”

“The selection is eighteen days away.”

“I know that too.”

A few heads turned toward him. Jian heard the edge in his own voice and forced it down.

“Then you understand the problem,” she said.

“I understand the dates. I don’t accept that the dates settle the matter.”

His father’s eyes shifted toward him for the first time.

Guowei leaned back slightly. “What do you propose?”

Jian had been waiting for the question.

“A substitution after registration, if the order allows one. A deferment until the selection. Provisional recognition from Azure Mountain if they are willing to test me early. If sect disciples are exempt from clan levy obligations, then there may be a route through the recruitment hall before the reporting date.”

The room changed by only a degree, but the elders stopped waiting for him to finish complaining and began listening to what he was actually saying.

Jian took a small, hard satisfaction from the shift.

Shen Peng frowned. “You have not reached the Sixth Layer.”

“No.”

“Azure Mountain’s recruiters told us last year that Sixth Layer was the practical minimum for an applicant without exceptional roots.”

“The practical minimum is not a written prohibition.”

“Your roots are not exceptional.”

“I know.”

The words came more sharply than he intended.

He knew exactly what his roots were. Wood, Water, Earth. Three ordinary affinities broad enough to cultivate and slow enough that no elder had ever mistaken him for a prodigy. He did not need the room to remind him.

Guowei’s gaze remained level. “And if Azure Mountain refuses?”

“Then I return before registration and nothing has changed.”

“Something changes,” Shen Gao said. “We lose three days preparing you for service while you chase a possibility.”

“I have spent a year preparing for another possibility. Three days is not much beside that.”

“To you.”

The reply came from Guowei.

Jian looked at him.

The patriarch rested one hand beside the three bamboo slips. “That is the part you continue to calculate from your own position. The clan has obligations that do not disappear because one of its members has a better use for his time.”

“And the clan’s interest in Azure Mountain?”

“Is why Rui remains.”

The answer landed harder than Jian expected.

No malice. No raised voice. No attempt to humiliate him. Just the reasoning, stated plainly.

Jian’s eyes moved toward Rui.

His cousin was sitting three places away, shoulders rigid beneath his robe. Rui did not look relieved. If anything, he looked ill.

That made it worse.

“So one candidate is enough,” Jian said.

“One candidate with the strongest likelihood of success is the risk we are willing to protect,” Guowei replied.

Jian looked down at the third bamboo slip.

His own name seemed very small written there.

“He’s injured.”

Shen Yao’s voice cut across the room.

Every head turned.

His father pushed away from the wall and stepped forward. “He came back through the gate bleeding badly enough to soak the bandage. You have not even asked whether he can march.”

“I can,” Jian said automatically.

Yao looked at him.

Jian wished immediately that he had kept his mouth shut.

“That is not the point,” his father said.

Guowei’s expression did not change. “Doctor Cao can examine him tomorrow. If the injury prevents service, the order provides for medical exemption.”

“And if it does not?”

“Then it does not.”

Yao came another step forward. His old injury made the movement uneven if someone knew to look for it. Most people did not. Jian always did.

“Send me.”

Silence settled harder than before.

Jian turned fully toward him.

Guowei’s eyes narrowed. “You are not on the eligible list.”

“I am Foundation Establishment. I have military service. I know the western routes better than half the officers they will put over these boys.”

“You are also responsible for the clan’s external security, and your leg has not survived a full day’s march in eight years.”

“It will survive enough.”

“Enough for what? To leave Qingyuan without collapsing? To reach the staging camp? To fight?”

Yao’s face tightened.

The patriarch’s voice remained quiet. “Your willingness is not in question. Your usefulness here compared with your usefulness there is.”

Jian almost laughed, though nothing about it was funny.

The same blade, turned toward someone else.

Yao looked toward the bamboo slip bearing Jian’s name.

“He has worked for this selection since last autumn.”

“I know.”

“He is close to Sixth Layer.”

“I know.”

“Then choose someone else.”

For the first time, something tired passed across Guowei’s face.

“Whom?”

Yao did not answer immediately.

The patriarch gestured toward the hall. “Name him.”

No one moved.

Jian looked along the rows and understood what Guowei had done. It was not a trick. There was no clean answer hiding beneath the discussion.

Shen Tao, whose absence could cost the clan caravan contracts and silver. Shen Min, whose marriage tied the family to stronger medicinal interests. The two southern brothers, either of whom could serve, but whose absence would leave the other carrying the entire southern tenancy through harvest, while sending both would strip that branch of every adult cultivator. The young father from the eastern branch whose wife had given birth less than a month ago. Rui.

There were other names. Every name came attached to something.

So did Jian’s.

His father knew it too.

That knowledge did not make Yao step back.

“I don’t care who,” he said.

Guowei’s gaze hardened.

Jian did step in then.

“Father.”

Yao did not look at him.

“Father.”

This time he did.

Jian wanted to say that he did not need rescuing. That would have been a lie. He wanted very badly to be rescued. He wanted his father to find some argument no one else had considered and tear his name from the bamboo strip.

But not by putting another name there without reason.

“Let me check the exemptions first,” Jian said.

His father’s mouth flattened.

“If there’s a lawful way around this, I can find it before registration. If there isn’t, we will know that too.”

“We?”

“Yes.”

Something in Yao’s expression shifted, not quite approval and certainly not acceptance.

Guowei looked between them. “You may investigate whatever provisions you like. The clan will not obstruct you.”

Jian turned back toward the patriarch. “And if Azure Mountain agrees to test me early?”

“Bring me recognition that changes your legal status before the registration deadline, and the council will reconsider the third name.”

That was more than Jian had expected.

Not much more.

But it was a door.

“And substitution?”

“If the county regulations allow it and someone eligible agrees to take your place, we will hear the request.”

“Deferment?”

“The same.”

Jian nodded once.

Aunt Qiao closed her ledger halfway. “Do not mistake permission to investigate for a promise that we will approve whatever you find.”

Jian gave a single nod.

The elders moved on after that because the clan still had two other people to equip, households to notify, money to allocate, work assignments to change, and five days in which to do all of it. Jian sat again, but he heard little of the discussion that followed.

He was already sorting routes in his head.

The county office maintained levy regulations in the Administrative Ward. The Shen Clan’s copy of the order might summarize exemptions without reproducing the implementing rules. The West Garrison would have registration procedures, but going there first risked wasting half a day in a queue. Azure Mountain’s Recruitment Hall opened after first bell and normally dealt with applications, recommendations, and preliminary assessments.

Azure Mountain first.

If the sect would recognize him before the levy took effect, nothing else might matter.

If they would not, then he would go to the county clerks.

He had five days.

Eighteen days until the life he had planned.

Five until another one claimed him.

When the meeting finally ended, people rose slowly, breaking into family groups and quiet conversations. Shen Kaiyuan’s younger brother clasped his shoulder. Shen Wen’s sister had begun crying before she reached him. Jian’s mother remained near the rear of the hall, her hands folded so tightly that the knuckles had gone pale.

Rui intercepted Jian before he could reach her.

“Jian.”

Jian stopped.

For once, Rui seemed unable to find the next sentence easily.

“I didn’t know they were going to—”

“Neither did I.”

“If there’s something I can—”

“There isn’t.”

The answer came too quickly.

Rui flinched as though Jian had struck him.

Jian closed his eyes for half a breath.

Rui had not written the third name. Rui had not ordered the levy. Rui had done nothing except be better positioned for the opportunity Jian wanted.

Knowing that did not stop the bitterness from arriving.

“I have to go to the Recruitment Hall tomorrow,” Jian said, making his voice less sharp. “If they allow an early assessment, I may still have a route out.”

Rui nodded. “Then I’ll go with you.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Because the sight of Azure Mountain’s favored applicant beside him while he begged for recognition would make Jian want to put his fist through a wall.

He did not say that.

“Because if they think I’m relying on your prospects to pressure them, it weakens the request. I need them to consider me on my own standing.”

That was even partly true.

Rui studied him for a moment, then nodded again. “All right.”

Jian moved past him.

His mother met him at the doorway and immediately looked down at his leg. “You went into the hills alone again.”

It was such an ordinary complaint that for one dangerous moment Jian thought she might cry. Shen Lihua did not. She crouched instead, touched two fingers lightly to the clean edge of the bandage, and studied the way he favored the leg when he stood. “Doctor Cao before first bell tomorrow,” she said. “Whatever else happens, I want that wound looked at properly.”

“I still mean to go to the Recruitment Hall.”

“I assumed you did.” She rose and adjusted the fall of his outer robe where it had twisted around the bandage. “You have never been good at leaving a closed door alone.”

Jian glanced toward the hall. Voices had already resumed behind the doors, the elders moving on to money, equipment, and the two other households that would have to prepare someone for service. “Do you think they chose correctly?” he asked.

Lihua was silent long enough that he regretted asking. “I think your granduncle looked at the clan and chose the loss he believed would damage it least. I also think a decision can be reasonable to the person making it and still be cruel to the person forced to live with it.”

The anger in Jian had nowhere useful to go against an answer like that. He looked down at the fresh binding around his thigh. “Father wanted him to name someone else.”

“Your father wanted your name gone.” Her mouth tightened, almost a smile and not one at all. “Wanting you spared and wanting someone else sent are not the same request.”

Behind the closed doors, someone began listing medicine costs. Lihua listened for a moment before returning her attention to him. “If you are going to the Recruitment Hall, go because you have something to ask of them, not because you need someone outside this family to tell you the council was wrong.”

“I am going because I am not finished.”

“I know.”

For a moment neither of them spoke. Jian had expected advice from her, some route through the problem that the elders had overlooked because Shen Lihua understood favors, marriages, debts, and the quiet movement of money better than most of the men in the hall. Instead she rested a hand against his sleeve, and the absence of an answer unsettled him more than another argument would have.

“Doctor Cao first,” she said. “After that, do what you intended to do.”

Jian nodded.

Outside, the courtyard had gone dark.

The clan servants had lit lamps beneath the covered walkways, and somewhere beyond the wall Qingyuan continued with an evening that had nothing to do with the Shen Clan’s three bamboo slips. A vendor called the last of his steamed buns along Ancestral Avenue. Someone laughed in the neighboring compound. A cart rattled toward the East Market. The city had not noticed that Jian’s year had just been divided into before and after.

He crossed the training courtyard slowly because of his leg.

Rui’s practice spear still rested against the weapon rack where he had left it that morning.

Jian stopped beside it.

Eighteen days remained until Azure Mountain’s selection.

Five remained until registration.

For the first time that evening, the five days before registration seemed heavier than the eighteen before selection.

He went inside, took out paper, ink, and the clan’s copy of the levy order, and began making a list of every authority in Qingyuan that might have the power to change his name on that third bamboo slip.

At the top, he wrote:

Azure Mountain Recruitment Hall.
