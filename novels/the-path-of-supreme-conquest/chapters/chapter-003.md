# Chapter 3: Every Door

Doctor Cao kept his surgery above a lamp-oil shop on Scholars' Lane, and he opened the door before Jian finished knocking.

"Sit. Leg on the stool, flat. Your mother sent word at midnight. I was awake anyway."

The room smelled of camphor and cold tea. Brush pots crowded the window ledge beside rows of stoppered jars. Cao was past sixty, white-browed, with ink on two fingers and a habit of humming one flat note through his nose while he worked. He cut away the binding, bent until his nose nearly touched the wound, and pressed the edges with a thumb.

"Two coins deep. Another finger and you would have brought me the leg in a basket. What bit you?"

"A boar."

"Naturally." He threaded a bone needle. "Hold the bench."

The first stitch went in. Jian held the bench.

"The county order allows an exemption for injury," he said, after the third.

The humming stopped.

"It does. The county physician must certify the injury bars service for thirty days." Cao drew the thread through. "This will close in nine. It will ache for a month. It will not stop you walking, and the county physician will not pretend otherwise."

"Write the certificate anyway. Say I cannot walk."

"I could." Cao tied off the knot and clipped the thread. "At registration the county's man will unwind your bandage, press this thigh, and read my signature aloud to the room. I am sixty-three years old. I am fond of my license."

Jian said nothing. His eyes measured the wound without permission: a finger's width, another, the extra depth a tusk would have needed to put him in a bed instead of a marching column. He fixed his gaze on the ceiling beams until the arithmetic stopped. It took longer than he wanted.

Cao smeared salve across the stitches and wrapped fresh cloth with quick, hard turns, and said nothing about what he had seen.

"Wrist."

Jian offered it. Cao's fingers settled over the pulse and stayed there. His brows drew together.

"I cannot see qi. I leave that to cultivators. A pulse I can read. Yours runs like two porters shoving through one gate. Sleep?"

"Five hours."

"Meals?"

"Enough."

"Rest days?"

Jian studied the jars on the ledge.

Cao released the wrist. "Sleep eight. Steep the grass, never boil it. Boil clearbreath and you have brewed hay." He nodded at the cloth bundle in Jian's lap. "Clean cuts. Crowns left standing. Whoever taught you to gather taught you well. Pity the lesson did not reach the rest of you."

"I have four days."

"You have four nights. Sleep through them."

He scrawled a note, pressed his seal beneath it, and folded it into Jian's hand: wound dressed, fit for ordinary march. "Keep it. Honest paper outlasts clever paper. Nobody believes this until the clever paper fails."

Jian paid from the pouch in his sleeve and stepped out into Scholars' Lane as the first bell rolled over Qingyuan from the Administrative Ward tower, one long iron note that rattled the lamp-oil shutters. Hall doors across the city would be opening. He tucked Cao's note inside his robe beside the copy of the levy order and set off toward the Azure Mountain Recruitment Hall, favoring the leg and in no mood to sleep.

The Azure Mountain Recruitment Hall stood two streets from the county office, a narrow building of blue-gray stone with a mountain peak painted above its door. Jian had come here eleven times in a year. Every visit had found a steward dozing behind an empty counter, a rack of application scrolls, and a bench with room for forty.

This morning the line ran out the door and down the steps.

Outer disciples filled the courtyard in blue-gray robes, white bands at the cuffs, standing in ranks of ten while stewards walked the rows with ledgers. Porters hauled crates from carts at the gate. Each crate carried two seals: the sect's mountain mark and, beside it in red, the stamp of Great Liang. Near the well, a young disciple argued with a quartermaster over ration tablets. Forty days, the disciple insisted. Thirty, said the quartermaster, and the paper agreed with the quartermaster.

A board by the door listed names in columns. Jian read the headings before he read any name. Rotation Two. Rotation Three. West Garrison. Yunling Pass. East Depot.

The sect he had spent a year approaching was packing for the same war.

He joined the line behind a woman clutching an application scroll and her son, a boy of fifteen rehearsing his birth date and his layer under his breath. The line moved in short, tired steps. Near the counter a clerk called a name and held out a folded slip.

"Outer Disciple Su Qinghe. Your posting."

The disciple who stepped forward was Jian's age or close to it, narrow through the shoulders, hair bound tight with undyed cord. The robe sat on him as if cut from his measurements. Jian had priced the cloth in his head a dozen times, the weave, the dye, the white band at the cuff, and had imagined those sleeves on his own arms so often that seeing them on another man came close to theft.

Qinghe's qi sat in him like water in a full jar, at least a layer above Jian's own, with no leak at the rim and no strain at the seams. Jian's own circuit had jammed at the sixth station every night for a month.

Qinghe unfolded the slip, read it, and read it again. His lips pressed thin. The clerk's brush was already moving toward the next name. Over the clerk's shoulder, on the posting list, Jian saw Qinghe's name written beneath the heading West Garrison.

Qinghe folded the slip in half and again and slid it into his sleeve. When he turned from the counter his gaze crossed Jian's, dropped to the bandaged thigh, and settled on the copy of the levy order in Jian's hand, the red wax plain on its fold. He studied it for the length of a breath, stepped around the line, and walked out without a word.

Jian watched the white cuff band cross the courtyard and vanish into the street. For a year he had believed those sleeves marked a man who answered to no clan. A clerk had written the man's name under a garrison heading.

Nearly an hour passed before Jian reached the counter.

Steward Ge ran the hall. He was past forty, a Foundation Establishment cultivator with a ledger-keeper's stoop and ink drying on his thumb. He recognized Jian before Jian spoke.

"Shen Jian. Fifth Layer, three roots, seventeen. Your scroll arrived in spring."

"It did." Jian laid the copy of the levy order on the counter with the red wax toward the steward. "My clan owes three cultivators, and I am the third name. Registration is in four days. Selection is in seventeen. I ask to be assessed early, or recognized provisionally, so the levy cannot reach me."

Ge read the order from the heading to the seal, then read its last paragraph again. The line behind Jian shifted without complaint. Most of them had come to ask the same thing in different words.

"I will answer in order," Ge said. "Early assessment first. Three elders from the mountain run the selection. They reach Qingyuan the evening before and leave the morning after, and the testing array travels with them. This hall holds scrolls and a seal. If I wished to test you, I could not."

"Write to the mountain. Ask for the examiners early."

"Three days up, three days down, and the answer would be this one. The elders do not travel for a single candidate."

"Provisional recognition."

"Does not exist." Ge set down his brush. "A man is on the roll or off it. The county reads the roll and nothing else, and it reads the roll as it stood on the date of the order. Enroll you this minute and the county would still date you from yesterday."

Jian tried the other end of the problem. "A letter from this hall stating I am an applicant in good standing."

"I can write it. It will say what is true. You are an applicant. The county will file it beside every other applicant's."

Ge leaned on the counter. "You're asking Azure Mountain to quarrel with the county over one untested applicant." He jerked his chin toward the courtyard. "You walked through the yard. Our own disciples are going west."

Jian's first reply died in his throat. It was a sound argument, and it stood against him.

"If I reach the Sixth Layer before selection?"

"You sit the assessment with every other applicant, in Qingyuan, on the day."

"While the levy holds me on the West Road."

"Yes."

Ge hesitated, then lowered his voice. "Men have entered the roll later through service merit and a commander's recommendation. Seldom. I promise nothing."

"A promise to nobody."

"A fact, offered because it exists. I do not expect it to comfort you."

Jian stood with his weight on the good leg. Every sentence the steward had spoken was true, which made each one impossible to argue with. "Put the refusal in writing. Under the hall's seal."

Ge's brows rose a fraction. "For whom?"

"My patriarch will want to read it. So will the county."

Ge wrote four lines in a quick, regular hand, stamped the paper twice, and held it by the corners to dry. "Carry it flat. The seal cracks."

"Thank you, Steward."

"Do not thank me." Ge was already reaching for the next scroll. "Win your Sixth Layer, Shen Jian. Whatever else happens, win it."

On the steps outside, Jian unfolded the list he had begun the night before. Azure Mountain Recruitment Hall stood at the top in his own hand. Two names followed it, and the last, set lower and smaller than the rest, belonged to a man he had hoped not to need. He drew a line through the first and walked toward the Administrative Ward.

The county office occupied three courtyards behind a gate guarded by two soldiers who had stopped noticing petitioners an hour after dawn. The levy desk stood in the second courtyard, under an awning, where eight clerks sat in a row facing a line of households that wound twice around a stone lion.

Jian drew the fourth clerk from the left, a narrow-faced young man of twenty-four with spotless cuffs, who had ruled a sheet of paper into four columns before Jian sat down.

"Yu Fang," the clerk said. "Four questions, I expect. Exemption, deferment, substitution, appeal. Everyone asks the same four. Ask them in that order and I will save you an hour."

Jian had arranged his own questions in that order the night before. He kept his face still. "Exemption."

Yu Fang opened a bound volume tabbed with three colors of thread. "Three classes. Persons enrolled on a recognized sect's roll before the date of the order. Officials and serving soldiers. Persons certified by the county physician as unfit for thirty days." He tapped each line without hurrying. "Which are you?"

Jian set down Steward Ge's refusal and Doctor Cao's note.

Yu Fang read the hall's letter twice and the doctor's note once. "Your physician is honest. Mine would not certify this leg either. I have turned away four worse since dawn. The steward's letter is correct. I will append it to your file, and it will change nothing in the file."

"Deferment."

"Granted to persons enrolled in a recognized institution and in term. Applicants are not enrolled." He read the line aloud, flatly, as written. "If the county deferred applicants, every household in Yunling would discover an applicant by tomorrow. The order assigns a number. Numbers do not bend for hope."

Jian made his jaw unclench. "Substitution."

"Lawful." Yu Fang turned two pages. "One assessed member may be replaced by another registered member of the same household, of equal or higher layer, named by the clan. Not a hired outsider. The order is a duty of persons, not of purses."

"Who names the substitute?"

"The clan. Neither of us."

The door existed, and someone else held the latch.

"Appeal."

"Against the assessment, by the clan, within three days of the order, and only on error of fact. The clan files appeals. Individuals do not." Yu Fang's brush hovered over his fourth column. "Your clan reported nineteen registered cultivators and owes three. I checked the figure against the registry this morning. It is correct."

Jian studied the stone lion's chipped paw until his face behaved. "Is there discretion?"

"There is." Yu Fang lowered his voice. "The Magistrate decides. He's leaving by winter. He won't sign anything the province might question."

"And you?"

Yu Fang glanced at his papers. "I'd like to work for the province one day. So no."

"You answered four questions in eight minutes."

"Sixty households have asked since dawn. I keep the answers ready." For the first time something near sympathy crossed the clerk's narrow face, then left it. "If you find a lawful route I have missed, bring it here. I would rather file it than miss it."

Jian paid the copying fee and took the exemption schedule, stamped twice. At the foot of the stone lion he unfolded his list. The second name came off with one line.

Only the last name remained, set lower and smaller than the others.

The sun had dropped behind the western wall by the time Jian crossed the Shen compound's threshold. A maid told him the patriarch sat in his study with Aunt Qiao and the quarterly accounts. Jian waited in the corridor until the door opened and Qiao came out with a ledger held against her chest. She glanced at his leg, at the papers in his sleeve, and held the door a moment longer than she needed to. She said nothing.

Guowei's study held a desk, two chairs, a shelf of ancestral tablets, and a brazier turned low. A cup of tea cooled at the patriarch's elbow. He did not offer Jian a seat, and Jian did not take one.

"Say it."

"I will give it in order, Patriarch." Jian laid each paper on the desk as he named it. "The Recruitment Hall. The steward cannot test me, cannot enroll me past the date of the order, and will not shield an applicant while his sect fills the same levy. His refusal, under the hall's seal." He set it flat. "Doctor Cao. The wound is shallow. He will not certify it, and the county physician would not either. His note." Another paper. "The county levy desk. Exemption, deferment, and appeal are closed to me. Substitution is lawful, and the clan names the substitute. I will not name one."

Guowei read nothing. He listened with both hands flat on the desk, and when Jian finished, the brazier ticked twice in the silence.

"You have come to ask for a fifth."

"A fine. The order lets a household answer a shortfall with a penalty. If the clan pays for one missing cultivator..."

"Aunt Qiao has done that sum." Guowei's voice stayed even. "It exceeds our reserves. It summons a county audit, and the audit reaches the Luo contracts and, through them, Shen Min's marriage. I weighed it before the council did. It was the first option I discarded."

"Then you have answered the question I came to ask."

"I have answered the question the law asks. You are asking another." Guowei turned the cup a quarter turn on the desk. "You ask whether this clan will spend itself to keep one member out of a column. It will not. It would not for Yao, who is the best man in this house, and it will not for you."

Jian's hands closed at his sides. He opened them again. "In the hall you said the council would reconsider if I brought recognition to change my status."

"I did."

"I brought proof none exists."

"You did, and you brought it honestly. I do not pretend otherwise." The old man's gaze held his. "You went to the sect, the county, the law, and last to me. You went in order, with paper, and you cheated no one. Had you tried it any other way, I would think less of you. I would still give the same answer."

"Every door told me the truth today."

"And they stayed shut."

Jian studied the three papers on the desk. Steward Ge's seal. Cao's flourish. The county's double stamp. A day's work, laid flat on lacquer, and every sheet of it argued for the patriarch. He had done the work correctly. He had verified each claim with his own hands. The correct work had closed every route, and he stood in the study with nothing left to check.

"What would change your answer?" Jian said. "Name the price, and I will find it."

Guowei regarded him for a long moment. For the first time that day, Jian saw how old the patriarch had become.

"There is one price," Guowei said. "I did not set it. Neither did the county." His thumb pressed the rim of the cup. "When you possess enough strength to refuse, you may refuse."
