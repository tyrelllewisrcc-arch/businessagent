# Chapter 4: What the Clan Can Afford

Jian reached the training courtyard before the cooks lit the stoves. Cao had prescribed eight hours of sleep. Jian had managed five and counted the difference as discipline.

He lowered himself onto the flagstones with the stiff leg stretched out and the spear across his knees, closed his eyes, and drew. The courtyard's thin qi came as it always did, a cold thread first, next a cord. At the third station the wound pulled, one hot wire from hip to knee, and the cord went slack. He opened his eyes, breathed out, and began again.

Footsteps crossed the flagstones. Shen Rui came in dressed, hair bound, boots laced, and passed his own stone without a glance. He stopped in front of Jian with his hands at his sides.

"I need to ask you something."

Jian studied his cousin's face for the pose. A good deed had a posture, and Rui, who did everything well, would hold this one well. He found none. Rui's eyes were red-rimmed and his collar hung crooked, as though he had dressed in the dark and walked the courtyard twice before he could stand still.

"Ask."

"The order lets somebody take your place. Guowei said so, in the hall." Rui swallowed. "Equal layer or higher. I'm Seventh. I want to do it."

Jian waited for the rest, the clause where Rui explained how little it would cost him. It did not come.

"The patriarch will refuse," Jian said.

"Probably."

"So the offer costs you nothing."

Rui flinched. It was small, a tightening at the corner of his mouth, and Jian was ashamed of how much he had wanted to see it.

"It costs me the intake," Rui said. "I'm nineteen before the next one. Same as you." He drew a breath through his nose. "You said it to the council and I sat there counting the dates. If it were my name on the slip, I'd say it wasn't fair either."

"If this is pity..."

"It isn't pity."

Jian said nothing.

Rui rubbed one hand across the other. "If I stay, I sit the selection. And you're on the West Road because they kept me here."

"That wasn't your decision."

"No." He turned his face to the weapons rack. "Doesn't help. I'd put the robes on every morning and think about it. So some of this is for me."

Jian listened for the performance. A performance would have been clean. Rui had handed him the selfish part unpolished, and the weight of the offer sat plainly between them.

Sixteen days to selection. Three to registration. He did the sum before he could stop himself: two weeks in Qingyuan, a Sixth Layer won in this courtyard while the leg closed, a place on the mountain's bench beside the boy he was about to dismiss. His mouth had gone dry.

He wanted it. The wanting arrived whole and shameless, and pride took up very little room beside it.

"Ask them," he said.

Rui's face opened. "You accept?"

"I said ask. I did not say it would work." Jian planted the spear butt and used the shaft to rise. The leg held. "When?"

"Second bell, in the front hall. I asked the elders to assemble." Rui hesitated. "Come with me. I will not do this behind you."

Jian nodded once and crossed the courtyard with the spear for a staff. Rui matched his pace without being asked.

The front hall held four elders and a pot of tea nobody was drinking. Guowei sat at the head of the table, Gao and Peng to his right, Aunt Qiao to his left with the ledger open before her. Jian took the place beside the door, where the draft from the corridor reached his bad leg. Rui walked to the center of the floor and bowed to the patriarch at the full depth, the one reserved for requests the speaker expects to lose.

"Patriarch. The levy order permits an assessed member to be replaced by another of the same household, of equal layer or higher. I am Seventh Layer. I ask to stand in Shen Jian's place, and I ask it in front of him."

Gao's brows drew together. Qiao's brush stopped over the page.

Guowei turned his head the width of a hand. "Jian. Do you ask this?"

The question had one honest answer, and Jian paid for it in front of four elders.

"I ask it," he said.

Guowei nodded, as though confirming a figure. "Aunt Qiao."

Qiao turned a page. "Rui's instruction has cost the clan more than every other young cultivator in the courtyard together. He is Seventh Layer at eighteen. His roots test above anyone this family has produced in two generations. If Azure Mountain accepts a Shen this year, the sum says it is Rui, and the sum says so by a distance." She did not raise her eyes. "If he goes west, he returns after selection. The mountain keeps no places for the absent. If he does not return, the clan loses the only seat it expects to fill. If he returns, the clan has spent its best candidate's last intake on a garrison."

"Last intake," Guowei repeated. He rested a hand on the table, the way he had beside the bamboo slips. "Rui. You are eighteen."

"Yes, Patriarch."

"You will be nineteen before the next selection."

"Yes."

"You'd spend the one door you will ever have on a war," Guowei said, "to buy a boy a door that may not open for him."

"I've thought about it."

"You understand what it costs you," Guowei said. "You haven't considered what it costs the clan. A levy takes one name. I'm not sending two futures west to save one."

"It's my future."

"It isn't." Gentler now. "Everything you are was paid for by this clan. Your instruction. Your medicine. Your hours in that courtyard. Repay it on the mountain. That's the payment I'll take."

Rui's hands closed at his sides.

Guowei's gaze moved to the door. "It's arithmetic, Jian. I won't call it kind."

Jian said nothing. He had expected the refusal. He had not expected how little relief would come with it.

"The request is refused," Guowei said.

Rui bowed again, lower, and stayed bent a moment longer than courtesy required. A single tear fell from one eye and darkened the floorboard between his boots. Qiao closed the ledger. "He is right, Rui," she said quietly. "I wish the sum said otherwise."

Jian was through the door before Rui straightened. The corridor ran cool and empty. He walked it with the spear for a staff and heard quick steps behind him, followed by his name.

"Jian."

He stopped, though he did not turn.

"I'm sorry," Rui said. His eyes were wet, and he made no attempt to hide them.

"For what? You asked."

"It's not enough."

"It's more than anyone else did." Jian turned. "Thank you, Rui."

The words were correct. They came out like a receipt.

Rui flinched as he had in the courtyard, and this time Jian watched it happen and took no pleasure in it. For a year he had kept a quiet tally of what Rui did well. Today Rui had added the one entry Jian could not dispute, and the sum was unbearable.

Lihua came for him at dawn with a wicker basket on her arm, which meant the day had a list.

The Crafts Ward woke early. Smiths fed their forges while dyers hung the first bolts across the lanes, indigo and madder-red against the gray. Peng Shou's leatherworks sat between a cooper and a potter, a low shop that smelled of tallow and wet hide. Peng Shou knelt over a boot last with a mouthful of tacks. He spat them into his palm when he saw her.

"Madam Shen. You are the fourth household this morning." He did not seem glad about it.

"Two pairs," Lihua said. "Marching boots, army pattern, one pair a size large for wraps. And a spear sling, good leather, double-stitched."

Peng Shou named a price. Jian had stood beside his mother in this shop since the age of nine, and he heard it for the first time as an insult.

"A third more than when I last came," Lihua said.

"A third more than yesterday." Peng Shou set down the last. "Quarter up last week. Half again by registration, and the garrison'll buy every pair I can't sew by then. Hate the war, Madam. Hate it. Daughter's marrying in spring, lease is due midwinter, and the war's been good to both, so I hate it quietly." He opened his hands. "I don't set the price. The garrison does, buying everything."

"I remember who sent fever pills to your door last spring," Lihua said, "when the girl couldn't keep water down. And who waited till autumn for the coin."

Peng Shou's mouth worked. The price dropped by a tenth. Jian counted the drop as it fell and understood he had watched his mother spend something no purse could carry.

At Nine Pines Pharmacy she bought wound powder, fever pills, and a roll of clean binding, and last of all a small flat packet she tucked into the basket without naming. The pharmacist weighed each item twice and wrote nothing down.

"The clan pays for this?" Jian asked on the walk home.

"The council has not settled what it adds to the army's issue. Aunt Qiao argues a quarter. Shen Gao argues nothing." Lihua shifted the basket to her other arm. "I'm not waiting on them to decide what you're worth while boots double."

"So the coin is yours."

"A mother's coin is not the clan's coin." She said it without heat, which closed the subject more completely than heat would have.

They walked a street in silence. A cart of bedrolls rolled past toward the West Barracks District, the driver whistling.

"Did they price me?" Jian asked.

"They priced everyone." Lihua did not slow. "You will carry the same issue and the same sum as Kaiyuan and Wen. They discounted your future. They did not discount your boots."

"You mean to comfort me."

"I mean to be accurate. Nobody said kind. It's even."

That evening she sat on the edge of his bed with the lamp beside her and his padded jacket across her knees, and sewed. Three small pouches went into the lining, one at the hem, one beneath the left arm, one low along the spine, and coin went into each in different weights.

"Never keep the whole of anything in one place," she said, and bit the thread.

"Yes, Mother."

"The garrison clerks send a courier packet to every column. Levy letters ride in it for a fee. Ten days if the column stays put, twelve if it does not." She knotted the thread. "Write when you can. Address it to the herb counter at the Exchange, in my name. I will write when there is news worth the fee."

Her needle stopped. The lamp hissed, and for three breaths nothing else made a sound. Her hand lay flat on the jacket, and Jian watched it and said nothing, and did not reach for it. After three breaths the needle resumed.

Shen Yao sent for him on the fourteenth day before selection, in the afternoon, to the storeroom behind the weapons rack.

Yao had spread a mat on the floor and emptied Jian's pack across it. Two spare shirts, the medicine roll Lihua had packed, a tin of salve, a writing kit, a bound copy of the Scripture's practical sections in Jian's own careful hand. His father sat on an overturned crate with the bad leg stretched out and a whetstone in his lap.

"Sit. Watch."

Jian lowered himself onto the mat. Yao lifted each item, weighed it in one hand, and set it on one of two piles.

"You packed for a man who expects to be comfortable. Two shirts are one too many. Boots are your life, and wraps are your boots' life. Four pairs of wraps. One on your feet, one drying against your body, two sealed in oilcloth. A man marching on wet feet is crippled by the third day, and an army does not count that as a casualty. It counts it as weather."

He weighed the Scripture copy and set it on the second pile. "Leave the paper."

"I have the sections by heart."

"Good. They weigh nothing in your head."

Yao picked up the spear and drew the whetstone along its head in short, even strokes. "Sling it by the balance, never by the head. Heavy things ride against the spine. Water on the left, so your right hand stays free. Drink before you are thirsty. Eat when food is in front of you, because the army will not always put it there."

Jian listened. His father spoke in the cadence of the drill yard, short sentences with the weight on the nouns, and Jian realized he had never heard it aimed at him.

"Now the rest." The stone rasped. "First lieutenant I had sent forty of us across a ford at dusk. I told him the river was up. He said the map showed a ford." Yao tested the edge with a thumb. "Eleven came out the other side."

"What happened to the lieutenant?"

"Wrote a good report." He shrugged. "He was only the one speaking. Tell him what you saw before you do what he says. Some of them thank you. The ones who don't, watch."

"And the men under him?" Jian asked. "The ones who obey?"

Yao's strokes slowed. "Watch them too. I had a captain once. His company marched for him on parade, loud as you like. First night the wagons didn't come, half of them were gone before dawn." The stone rasped. "Never mistake having power over someone for having their loyalty."

"You're telling me to disobey."

"I'm telling you you'll stand on one side of it, and probably both, before this war's done." He ran his thumb along the blade. "On the road, find the oldest private who isn't an officer and walk next to him. He's stayed alive for reasons the manuals don't list."

He held out the spear. Jian took it. The edge was true to the width of a hair, and the shaft carried the warmth of his father's hands.

Jian took longer to speak than he intended. "You offered to go."

"He refused."

"You offered."

"It changed nothing."

"It changed something."

Yao's hand stopped on the stone. For the space of two breaths he said nothing, and his eyes stayed on the lantern on the shelf.

"Eat," he said. "Sleep. Wash your feet."

Night came with the pack by the door and the spear lashed on top of it, the new sling already threaded through its rings. Jian did not sleep.

He went to the courtyard at midnight and lit one lamp on the weapons rack. Rui's stone sat empty ten paces away. Cao had said eight hours. Jian lowered himself onto his own stone, stretched the stiff leg out before him, and drew.

The courtyard's qi answered thin and cold. Fourth station, the split. Fifth. At the sixth station the left stream arrived and waited, and the right came late, and he seized them both, and they locked against his breastbone like a jammed gate.

Rui had explained his own crossing of the sixth station months ago, in this courtyard, and Jian could still repeat every word. None of the words had ever made the station open.

A line of the Scripture rose in his mind, the way verses always had.

*The stagnant pool preserves its water and becomes foul. The running stream loses itself with every moment and remains clear.*

He took it as an instruction. Pools stalled. Streams ran. His circuit had stalled at the sixth station every night for a month, and a stall was a pool. He cleared the silt, and before the last cycle had cooled he began the next, and the one after it, with no rest between, running the stream faster than the stream wanted to run.

He chalked a stroke on the flagstone beside his knee, using a stub from the weapons rack, for every run he broke. Each run had to hold twelve circuits without a stall. Twelve was the number he believed in. None of tonight's had passed the seventh. The leg throbbed in time with his pulse, and the qi in his chest packed thicker, silt over silt.

Somewhere past the fourteenth broken run he heard himself say, "It does not matter."

The courtyard took the words and gave nothing in return. Fourteen days to selection. Azure Mountain would test Rui and pass him, and Jian would be marching toward a garrison, and a Sixth Layer won tonight would open no door in Qingyuan before the West Garrison closed on him. It did not matter. He said it again, flatly, to the lamp.

He rubbed the chalk between his fingers and added another mark.

The cycle stalled at the sixth station. He ran it again. Qi dragged through his arms like wet rope, and sweat soaked his collar. When the first cook raked ash beyond the kitchen wall, the tally ran to thirty-one strokes and the Sixth Layer remained shut. His eyes burned. The leg pulsed from hip to knee.

He scrubbed the chalk from the stone with his sleeve and limped toward his room. The pack waited by the door, ready as his father had left it. He lay down in his clothes with the lamp still burning on the rack outside, and listened to the compound wake around him.

The decision stood. Registration began at first bell.
